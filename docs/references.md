## The ballparks

- **What:** the English and Japanese Wikipedia article of each of the 94 parks (30 current MLB parks, 44 past MLB parks, 12 NPB home parks and 8 other NPB grounds), at the revision named for each in [NOTICE.md](../NOTICE.md) and `scripts/parks.data.json`. Each gives the fence distances at the foul lines, the alleys and the middle: in feet for MLB, in metres (両翼, 中堅, 左右中間) for Japan.
- **Useful for:** each park's fence, drawn through its published distances; the names in English and Japanese; the teams.
- **May use:** the distances, which are facts. The articles are CC BY-SA 4.0 and none of their text, tables or pictures is used, so the share-alike condition does not reach the package; the articles are cited by revision so that the figures can be checked and credit is given.
- **How they were read:** a distance is taken as the article's label says (foul line, left-center, center) and placed at 45, 22.5 and 0 degrees from dead centre; where a park lists more than one figure between, they are spread in the order listed. This is our reading, since the articles give distances and not directions. Where an article has several periods (Kauffman Stadium's alleys changed for 2026) the current figure is used. Where the Japanese article gives announced and measured distances (Korakuen, Kawasaki) the measured are used. Japanese figures are in metres and converted once, at 3.280839895 feet to the metre.

### The past MLB parks

- **Which:** every ground an MLB club called home that the SPX Stats archive holds (its public `api.venue_place` and `api.venue_info` views, read in October 2026: 54 MLB rows), and the classic ones besides: Forbes Field, Ebbets Field, the Polo Grounds, Shibe Park, Crosley Field, Sportsman's Park, Griffith Stadium, Comiskey Park, Tiger Stadium, Baker Bowl, Braves Field, League Park, Cleveland Stadium, Municipal Stadium (Kansas City), Memorial Stadium, County Stadium, Candlestick Park, Seals Stadium, Wrigley Field (Los Angeles), 44 in all. Each has its `status` as `past`, and `from` and `to`, the first and last season an MLB club played there (read from the article's list of tenants; Robert F. Kennedy Memorial Stadium has two spells, 1962 to 1971 and 2005 to 2007, and the `from` and `to` span both).
- **The key to join on:** every park, current, past and NPB, has `wikidata`, the Wikidata item of its ground (`Q49136` for Fenway Park). Each was read as the `wikibase_item` that the cited article carries (MediaWiki API, `prop=pageprops`), then checked against the item's English label. Where SPX Stats holds the ground, its `api.venue_place.wikidata_id` is the same item. The items are unique across the 94 parks.
- **The last fence is the park's fence.** `points` is the fence the article gives as the park's last (or its only). Where the article dates earlier fences (Huntington Avenue Grounds moved center field from 530 to 635 feet in 1908; the Polo Grounds had three fences; Shibe Park four), each is a period in `periods` with its first and last season, and `drawKyogijo({ sport: "mlb", park, year })` draws the one in effect that season. A season in no period, or no `year`, gets `points`. Periods are only the complete fences that an article dates; a figure the article gives for a single year without the other distances (Shibe Park's 360 feet on opening day of 1909, Braves Field's fences moved nearly every year) is left out or noted, never filled in.
- **Choosing among an article's figures.** The infobox's set of five (left, left-center, center, right-center, right) is used when it has one. Where an article lists the posted distances of the whole wall (the Polo Grounds, Forbes Field, Sportsman's Park, County Stadium, Shea Stadium) those are used, in the order listed, spread between the foul lines and the middle as above. Where a deepest corner stands beside center (Sportsman's Park 426, Forbes Field's 442, Ebbets Field's notch at 399) the figure labelled center is center and the corner is read left of it. A park's article that gives one figure to a position and a different one in its text uses the infobox (Griffith Stadium's left-center, listed at 360 feet next to left field at 388, is as published). Four figures only (Baker Bowl, Huntington Avenue Grounds) or three (Exposition Park, Sick's Stadium) are drawn through just those; the curve does not invent a right-center.
- **Not guessed:** a park without published distances is left out. These grounds were looked for and are missing:
  - *League Park* in Cincinnati (`Q6508941`, Reds 1884 to 1901): the article gives left field, 253 feet, and nothing else.
  - *Palace of the Fans* (`Q7126271`, Reds 1902 to 1911): the infobox gives five figures, but its right field (450 feet) is longer than its center (400) and its left-center is 418; the labels look transposed and we do not correct them.
  - *Kingdome* (`Q990430`, Mariners 1977 to 1999), *Hard Rock Stadium* as Joe Robbie Stadium (`Q864339`, Marlins 1993 to 2011), *Bennett Park* (`Q4889721`, Tigers 1901 to 1911), *American League Park* in Washington (`Q4744320`), *West Side Park* (`Q3299098`, Cubs), *Eastern Park* (`Q5330371`), *Congress Street Grounds* (`Q5160829`), *Washington Park* in Brooklyn (`Q49577200`) and *South Side Park* (`Q7568485`, White Sox 1901 to 1910): the article gives no distances.
  - *Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum* (`Q849784`, Dodgers 1958 to 1961): left field, 251 feet, and the alleys, 320 feet; no center or right.
  - *Robison Field* (`Q11350605`, Cardinals 1893 to 1920): the article's figures for 1909 have no right field, and those of 1893 are for the ground before its rebuilding.
  - The 19th-century grounds of the National League and the American Association, the Federal League's, and the neutral or one-off sites of the regular season (Estadio de Béisbol Monterrey, the Tokyo Dome, Rickwood Field and so on) were not looked for.

### Walls, quirks and the extra points

- **What:** the same article of each park, at the revision `scripts/parks.data.json` names (the Japanese article for the walls of the parks of Japan), read again for what it says beyond the fence's distances: how high the fence stands (`walls`), a hill, an overhang, a pole, the bullpens, ivy and the basket (`quirks`), the distance to the backstop (`backstop_ft`), the warning track's width (`track_ft`), which dugout is on which line (`dugouts`), and further distances in the text (`labels`). Each fact is in the data with the seasons the article dates it; nothing is guessed, and a park whose article gives no figure keeps its plain drawing. The 90 parks that gained no fence point and no track are byte for byte what they were (a test holds each one's hash).
- **Which parks:** 35 have wall heights (24 of MLB, 11 of Japan), 43 a backstop distance, 5 quirks (`daikin-park`, `fenway-park`, `polo-grounds`, `tiger-stadium`, `wrigley-field`), 4 the sides of their dugouts, 1 a track width (`pnc-park`, 18 feet), 3 more points of the fence (`wrigley-field`, `sportsmans-park`, `milwaukee-county-stadium`).
- **Where a stretch stands (our reading):** the articles give heights by words (left field, the corner, the Baggie) and never by angle, so each wall's `from` and `to` are our reading, from the park's own points: left field runs from the foul line to the left-centre point (-45 to -22.5), centre field between the left-centre and right-centre points, right field from the right-centre point to the line (22.5 to 45); "left and centre field" is the two together, a corner the outer quarter of its field (-45 to -33.75 for Coors Field's left field corner, -45 to -30 for Wrigley Field's, where the wall begins to curve in), and a park that lists five heights (Oracle Park) has each stretch between its own points. A wall's `place` says which words the article used, and is what the drawing calls it unless the article names it. `labels` and a wall's angles are ours; the distances and the heights are the article's.
- **Where an extra point stands:** as the older points do (see above): the foul line at 45 degrees, the left-centre and right-centre points at 22.5, centre field at 0, and where an article lists more than one figure between, in the order listed and evenly spread (Sportsman's Park's twelve markers, with true centre field at dead centre; County Stadium's nine, every 11.25 degrees). Wrigley Field's two wells (357 feet where the left bleacher wall begins to curve in, "about 360" at the front of the well, 363 at the notch of the right-centre well) are read at -36, -30 and 30 degrees. Where the infobox and the text give different figures for one place the infobox is used, as before: Jarry Park's text says the centre field was 417 feet and posted as 420, and 420 is kept; Wrigley Field's centre field marker (400) stays at dead centre though its text puts true centre, unmarked, at about 390.
- **Conflicts:** Wrigley Field's infobox gives the corners as 15 feet and its text says they were reduced from 15 to 11 feet before 2015; the dated text is used (15 feet to 2014, 11 from 2015).
- **A hill's size:** Tal's Hill is "a 90 ft wide incline" at 30 degrees from 2000 to 2016. The article gives no depth and no direction, so the hill is drawn against the fence as wide across as it is long (90 by 90 feet) and centred on dead centre; its drawn fence is the park's today (409 feet), because the article gives only the old centre field figure of 436 feet and not the rest of that fence, so there is no period for it. Duffy's Cliff (Fenway Park, 1912 to 1933: "a 10 ft incline", with its wall at 25 feet) and Crosley Field's terrace (15 degrees) have no width or depth and are not drawn; Fenway's 25 foot wall of those seasons is.
- **The sizes of marks:** a dugout, a bullpen, a pole and the backstop's bar are marks for the eye. The articles give where they are and not how big they are.
- **Left out, with the reason:** the roof outlines of the Rogers Centre, the Astrodome (710 feet across), the Olympic Stadium and the Metrodome (no figure places a roof against the field); Camden Yards' and Coors Field's earlier wall heights that are only a rise ("raised 8 ft 9 in") and so give no figure for the old wall; heights with no stretch (loanDepot Park's "10 to 16 ft", Yokohama Stadium's 5.0 to 5.3 m, ES CON Field's "mostly 2.8 m", Hanshin Koshien Stadium's 3.0 m, which its article calls unpublished); Korakuen's 1.9 to 4.7 m and Rakuten Mobile Park Miyagi's partial 1.8 m; scoreboards in play (Crosley Field's 55 feet, Griffith Stadium's 41); Citi Field's 16 and 8 feet at the Home Run Apple (a home run line, not the wall); Yankee Stadium's (1923) distances by year, a partial list that does not date how long each stood; the Polo Grounds' 60 foot clubhouse wall in dead centre (the article gives no width for it), the Forbes Field roof, the Ebbets Field screen (no figure for the wall); and the warning track's width of every park but PNC Park's (no other article gives one). A park's foul territory is given only as the distance to the backstop; the articles' statements that it is small or large have no figure.

## The data layers

- **What was read:** the lists of what each plotting package draws, and how its charts look in its own gallery, to know what to match: [hockey_rink](https://github.com/the-bucketless/hockey_rink) (item 11: scatter, plot, arrow, wavy arrow, text, clipping to the boards) and [mplsoccer](https://github.com/andrewRowlinson/mplsoccer) (item 12: scatter, lines, comet lines, arrows, markers). [ggsoccer](https://github.com/Torvaney/ggsoccer) (item 13) and [sporty](https://github.com/sportsdataverse/sdvplot-js) (item 10) were read for how a layer sits on a surface that is a spec of a few numbers.
- **Ideas credited (MIT, so ideas freely and code with credit; we wrote our own):** mplsoccer's comet line (a line that fades in towards its end, with its width growing), its arrows and its split of a mark into a shape, a colour and a size from the data; ggsoccer's layers that take the pitch's own coordinates. No code of either is used: the curves, waves, heads and shapes in `src/layers.marks.ts` are plain geometry (quadratic curves, offsets along a normal).
- **Not used:** hockey_rink's code (GPL-3.0). Only the list of what it draws was read, from its README. sportypy and sportyR (GPL-3.0) were not read for this; their READMEs and licence files were read afterwards, for [the comparison](./compare.md), and only facts were taken.
- **Ours:** outcomes drawn by fill and dash as well as colour (a miss is hollow, a failed pass dashed), a layer's title, description and table for screen readers, a clip to each surface's own outline, and the team colours following a score drawing's teams.
- **Data:** the demo's shots, passes and balls in play are made up from a fixed seed (`demo/layers-data.js`). No real game, player or team is in the package.

### Heat maps, hexagons, zones and contours

- **What was read:** the lists of charts each package offers for a binned statistic: [hockey_rink](https://github.com/the-bucketless/hockey_rink) (item 11: `binned_statistic_2d`, `heatmap`, `hexbin`, `contour` and `contourf`, all clipped to the boards) and [mplsoccer](https://github.com/andrewRowlinson/mplsoccer) (item 12: `bin_statistic`, `heatmap`, `bin_statistic_positional` with `heatmap_positional` for the juego de posición zones, `hexbin`, `kdeplot`). [sporty](https://github.com/sportsdataverse/sdvplot-js) (item 10) was read for its NBA shot zones. The one file of mplsoccer read for a figure is `dimensions.py`, for where the positional zones' lines lie.
- **Ideas credited (MIT, so ideas freely and code with credit; we wrote our own):** mplsoccer's positional zones (lanes by bands, the lines at the penalty and goal areas and half way between the penalty area and the halfway line), its binned statistic of count, sum, mean, median or a function of the values with `normalize`, its heat map over a grid and its hexagon plot; sporty's zones of a shot chart; hockey_rink's clipping of every heat layer to the boards. The binning, the hexagon grid (axial coordinates, cube rounding), the Gaussian kernel density (Scott's rule), the highest-density levels and the marching squares in `src/heat.stats.ts` are written from their textbook definitions.
- **Not used:** hockey_rink's, sportypy's and sportyR's code (GPL-3.0): only hockey_rink's list of charts was read, from its README. No palette of any package is copied (the ramps are our own).
- **Ours:** the legend as a layer and an option, a ground that makes one ramp readable on every surface, the ramps' light and dark sets checked by a test, zone charts with a rate (a field-goal percentage) and the number of marks in each zone, a table of each layer's numbers and a description that names the hottest cells or zones.
- **Zone definitions:** the soccer, NBA, NHL, baseball and football zone sets, with each source, are in [sources.md](./sources.md#zone-sets); the NHL slot and home plate and the football pass edges are ours.

## The ready-made charts

- **What the charts are for:** the shot charts, shot maps, spray charts and pass charts that the plotting packages show in their galleries and READMEs (the lists recorded above under items 10, 11 and 12). Each of ours is one function that returns data layers, so what is drawn is the layer system's, not a new renderer.
- **Ideas credited (MIT, so ideas freely and code with credit; we wrote our own):** [mplsoccer](https://github.com/andrewRowlinson/mplsoccer) (item 12) draws shot maps with a mark sized by the expected goals of the shot and goals marked apart from the rest, and pass maps; [sporty](https://github.com/sportsdataverse/sdvplot-js) (item 10) draws an NBA shot chart with zones. Those are the ideas the NBA, NHL, football and soccer charts follow. No code of either is used.
- **Not used:** [GeomMLBStadiums](https://github.com/bdilday/GeomMLBStadiums)'s spray-chart geom (item 7, no licence) was not read: ours puts balls on the park of their season, past parks included, which it does not, from the fence figures in [sources.md](./sources.md). hockey_rink, sportypy and sportyR (GPL-3.0) were not read for this either.
- **Ours:** a hit type told by shape and size as well as fill; the balls that cleared the fence drawn beyond it, by the fence the park had that season; the pass chart's air yards from the line of scrimmage and its grid of completions; the goal-mouth view (the goal frame of the Laws, seen from the front with the shooter's left on the left, a mark where each shot crossed the goal line, in the unit the data is in); a key drawn in the margin from the chart's own marks; the zones' table, and every word of a chart in English and Japanese.
- **Data:** the demo's, the tests' and the docs' shots, balls and passes are made up from fixed seeds (`demo/charts-data.js`). No real game, player or team is in the package.

## The road to the final

- **What was read:** nothing of any package. The idea is the graphic that sports sites post for a World Cup final, "the road to the final": the two finalists' flags large at the top, the rounds named down the middle, each team's matches on its side as the two teams' round flags with the score between them and "FT" or "AET" under it, and the group stage as a row of small flags, three-letter codes and scores at the foot. The one that prompted this was Football Benchmark's "FIFA World Cup 2026 Road to the Final", seen as inspiration only.
- **Ideas credited:** that arrangement, and nothing more. No artwork, layout measurement, type, flag, crest or text of that graphic or of any other is used; the arrangement here is drawn from scratch, sized by the package's own estimates of text, for four sizes of its own.
- **Ours:** the round mark (a picture clipped to a circle with a ring, and the team's initials on its colour when there is none), the same mark on a pitch, a rink or a court in a score, the rounds as keys with words in English and Japanese and any label of the caller's, a series (`4-2`) as well as a knockout, the shoot-out written in the order the marks stand, the table that lists every match, and the accessible title and description.
- **Data:** the demo's, the tests' and the docs' nations, their flags and their results are made up (`demo/road-data.js`). Every flag is a few shapes in plain colours drawn in code, and none is a real national flag or emblem: the nations do not exist. No real team, player, result or flag is in the package.

## Team shape

- **What was read:** the titles of the pages of [mplsoccer](https://github.com/andrewRowlinson/mplsoccer)'s docs gallery (item 12), on 2026-10-13: Pass Network, Pass flow plot, Convex Hull, Plots Delaunay Tessellation of Players, Plots a Voronoi diagram, Formations, and the sonars (Sonar, Sonar Grid, Sonar Zones, Bin Statistic Sonar). That list is what the team layers match; no page, example or source of it was opened.
- **Ideas credited (mplsoccer is MIT, so ideas freely and code with credit; we wrote our own):** a pass network (a node at the average place of a player's passes, sized by touches, a link sized by the passes between two players, a minimum number of passes), the Voronoi diagram and the Delaunay triangulation of the players, the convex hull of a team, a flow field of the average direction of passes in each cell of a grid, and a sonar of direction roses (one for each player, cell or zone, counts or mean length by angle). The hull (Andrew's monotone chain), the triangulation (Bowyer and Watson's incremental method, with ghost triangles for the hull), the cells (the box cut by the bisector of each neighbour), the averages, the grid and the roses are written from their textbook definitions in `src/team.geometry.ts`, `src/team.network.ts`, `src/team.shape.ts` and `src/team.motion.ts`.
- **Not used:** hockey_rink's, sportypy's and sportyR's code (GPL-3.0); none was read for this. No code, data or palette of mplsoccer is in the package.
- **Ours:** the formations' table in registered frames (so one formation lies on a surface of any size and can be read back in a provider's numbers), a shift for baseball and the offensive and defensive sets of football, every layer cut to the surface's outline, a table of each layer's numbers and a description for a screen reader, and every word in English and Japanese. The share of the surface each team controls is counted on a grid kept to the surface, so a rink's rounded corners and a ballpark's fence are not counted.
- **Data:** the tests', the docs' and the gallery's players, passes and movements are made up from fixed seeds. No real game, player or team is in the package.

## Replay

- **What was read:** only what items 11 and 12 above say of themselves: [hockey_rink](https://github.com/the-bucketless/hockey_rink) (item 11) and [mplsoccer](https://github.com/andrewRowlinson/mplsoccer) (item 12) both animate tracking data on their surfaces. That one fact, that a timeline of positions played on a surface is a chart worth having, is all that was taken. Neither package's animation code or interface was opened.
- **Ideas credited (mplsoccer is MIT, so ideas freely and code with credit; we wrote our own):** a replay of tracking data on a pitch as a set of frames of positions by time. The model of a timeline (frames of `{ t, objects }` with an `id`, a `team` and a place each, and events at times), the interpolation between frames (a straight line, or a Hermite curve through the frames with Catmull-Rom tangents, from their textbook definitions), the trail, the state machine of the clock and the painter that moves only the marks that move are written for this package.
- **Not used:** hockey_rink's code (GPL-3.0). No code, data or palette of either package is in the replay.
- **Ours:** a frame as a string that is the same every time, built on the data layers; a controller that needs no page; controls in English and Japanese; steps for a reader who prefers reduced motion, a pause when the page is hidden and a live region for the events; a table of the events.
- **Data:** the demo's plays (a team brings the ball forward and scores, on the rink and on the pitch) are made up from a fixed seed (`demo/replay-data.js`). No real game, player or team is in the package.

## Surfaces through the years

- **What was read:** the pages named in [sources.md](./sources.md#surfaces-through-the-years), for facts only: a figure, the season it began in, what it replaced. Wikipedia is CC BY-SA 4.0 and none of its text, tables or pictures is used; it is cited by revision. Sports Reference and ESPN were not used, nor was the GPL code of sportypy, sportyR or hockey_rink.
- **Not guessed.** A change is in `src/eras.ts` only when a page read says it, with the season. These were looked for and not found, or found only in a source that gives no season or contradicts another, and are **left out** (the surface keeps today's marking in every season, or the nearest cited one):
  - *NHL goal crease.* A retail hockey site (hockeyresponse.com, no sources of its own) dates an L-shaped crease in 1933-34, 7 by 3 feet in 1939-40, 8 by 4 feet in 1951-52, a semicircle added in 1986-87, the half-moon replacing the rectangle in 1991-92 (as a search result for NHL Records' page also said), and the 1998-99 width of 8 feet. No primary source, no rulebook and no second independent page was read, so the crease is drawn in today's shape in every season.
  - *NHL goal line before 1997 and blue lines before 1998.* The same site says the goal line was about 10 feet from the end boards before 1990-91, 11 feet from then; it gives no source. Nothing was found for the blue lines' place before the 73 feet of 1998-99.
  - *NHL rink corner radius and the referee's crease.* No page read dates a change of the corner radius or the referee's crease; they are drawn as they are today in every season.
  - *NHL face-off circle hash marks before 2012-13, and the other leagues' hash marks.* The marks beside an end-zone circle were 3 feet apart through 2013-14 and 5 feet 7 inches from 2014-15 (NHL Official Rules 2012-13, Rule 1.9; the NHL and NHLPA release of 6 October 2014; CBS Sports, 6 October 2014): that is in the data (`nhl.hashApart.2012`, `.2014`). No rulebook older than 2012-13 was read (a search found none online), so earlier seasons are a gap drawn at 3 feet. The AHL, ECHL, OHL, WHL and PWHL print the marks 5 ft 7 in apart today but no page read dates when each began or moved them, so their marks are 5 ft 7 in in every season.
  - *The first blue lines and the first red goal lines.* "Two blue lines as of 1928-29" is read (Wikipedia, "Offside (ice hockey)"); the first season of the blue lines and anything before is not drawn differently.
  - *NBA court size.* 94 by 50 feet is the NBA's today; for the BAA's first seasons and for arenas that differed, no source was found. A 1924 rule that allowed 90 by 50 feet (a Livestrong history piece, not a rulebook) is not used.
  - *NBA lower defensive box, lane space marks, sideline (coaching box) marks and substitution marks.* The NBA's Rule 1 states them today; no page read gives the season each appeared (the box is tied to the restricted area, which is dated 1997-98, but no page says the marks came with it), so they are drawn in every season.
  - *NFL yard numbers, the end zone depth, the field's length, the 1932 playoff game's marks, the one-yard sideline ticks.* Not looked for beyond the pages above, or undated there. The NFL field is drawn 360 by 160 feet in every season.
  - *CFL field width, the hash marks before 2015, the numerals and the yard-line arrows* (Wikipedia says arrows came in the early 1980s: not drawn differently). The CFL's field is drawn 65 yards wide in every season.
  - *Baseball: the bases' size, the mound's diameter, the coaches' boxes, the running lane, the foul lines before 1931 (they ran on indefinitely), the keyhole path from the mound to the plate, the infield's grass and dirt.* Undated in the pages read, or style. The plate's place before 1861 and its shape before 1857 are unknown; they keep the nearest cited state.
  - *The 1893 to 1902 mound height.* The article says 15 inches "from 1903 through 1968" and, in another sentence, "from 1893 to 1950"; only 1903 is used.
- **Planned.** The CFL's 2027 field (100 yards between the goal lines, 15-yard end zones, posts on the end line) is announced and drawn for `year: 2027` and later, and marked `planned`. If the league changes its mind, the three spans in `src/eras.ts` are the place.

Pennsylvania, Montana and the other states' own dimensions were not found. The NCAA's width is read from its diagram only, and the try mark's size is not given.
- **Baseball and softball:** Little League's own rule text (in its phone app only), so the batter's boxes, plate and the Majors' infield arc are not drawn from it; Junior, Senior and Big League Little League (90-foot, 60 ft 6 in, as the NFHS and the MiLB), PONY's Colt, Palomino and Thorobred divisions (the same) and its Foal, Shetland and Pinto divisions (not drawn); Minor League Baseball's facility standards (not public); the NFHS softball rule text and base size (sold); the WBSC's fast-pitch rulebook (only a changes list was found); USA Softball's other divisions (girls', boys', men's, 16-inch and modified pitch: the table is in the 2026 rule book, one division of each game is drawn).
- **MLB's bases before 2023.** They are 15 inches square until 2022 and 18 from 2023 (`rulesFor("mlb", year).rules.baseSize`); how long 15 inches had been the size (CBS Sports: "well over a century") and any earlier size were not found, so no earlier change is dated.

## Styling and annotations

- **What was read:** the lists of what each package lets a caller style, from their READMEs and documentation pages as far as they say it: [sportypy](https://github.com/sportsdataverse/sportypy) (item 9: colour updates per named feature and a list of feature names), [ggsoccer](https://github.com/Torvaney/ggsoccer) (item 13: colour, fill, line width, line type and alpha, goal styles, a custom goal function, a direction label) and [mplsoccer](https://github.com/andrewRowlinson/mplsoccer) (item 12: stripes, z-order, padding, an axis and labels on a pitch).
- **Ideas credited (ggsoccer and mplsoccer are MIT, so ideas freely and code with credit; we wrote our own):** a direction arrow with a label, a caller's function that draws the goal, line width, dash and opacity as options, mown stripes in several patterns, and an axis with ticks and labels. The feature table, the looks' palettes, the line restyling, the mowing patterns and the layout of the words round a surface are written for this package.
- **Not used:** sportypy's code and its names for features (GPL-3.0); the package's own names come from its own surfaces. No palette of any package is copied: the looks' colours are chosen here and held to WCAG 2.2's contrast by a test. The mowing patterns are patterns that groundsmen use (alternate bands, a chequerboard, diagonals, rings, wedges), not any club's.
